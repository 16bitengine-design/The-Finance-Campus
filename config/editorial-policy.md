# Editorial policy: mindset, behaviour, urgency and hope

## Our purpose

The Finance Campus is not a financial-news channel or a market-data service. We help ordinary people build healthier money mindsets and make better everyday financial decisions. The subject is money, but the real focus is the human being making decisions about it.

Our videos should make viewers recognise a pattern, question an unhelpful belief, understand a consequence and leave with one practical change they can try.

## What matters most

Prioritise:
- Money beliefs learned from family, peers, culture and experience.
- Spending triggers, impulse buying, social pressure and lifestyle inflation.
- The difference between looking wealthy and building financial stability.
- Procrastination, avoidance, shame and fear around money.
- Patience, consistency, delayed gratification and long-term thinking.
- Habits that protect income, create breathing room and reduce avoidable stress.
- The emotional and practical cost of debt, financial dependence and living without a plan.
- Resilience when income is low, irregular or uncertain.
- Financial boundaries, goal-setting and intentional choices.
- The small decisions that compound into stronger or weaker financial lives.

Use relatable everyday situations and simple hypothetical examples. Explain only the financial concepts needed to make the human lesson clear. Do not overload a video with rates, tables, product comparisons, market updates, legal detail or statistics unless that information is essential to the lesson.

## The role of research

Research exists to keep the lesson honest, not to turn the channel into a data bulletin.

- Prefer evergreen educational material from central banks, regulators, government agencies, academic research and established financial education institutions.
- Use sources to check core principles, risks, terminology and country-specific rules.
- Use statistics only when they materially strengthen the lesson; explain them simply and identify their date and geography.
- Label hypothetical examples clearly. Never present an illustrative scenario as a real case or statistic.
- Do not build the editorial calendar around press releases, market movements or the volume of available financial news.
- Do not imply that a source supports a claim unless the relevant passage has actually been checked.
- Keep Ugandan or East African rules distinct from general global principles.

## Story structure: educate while creating urgency

1. Open with a recognisable money situation, belief or uncomfortable question.
2. Reveal the hidden habit, assumption or decision behind it.
3. Show how the pattern can affect someone's choices and future if it continues.
4. Explain the key money principle in plain language, using a simple visual example.
5. Offer a realistic action the viewer can take.
6. End with agency and hope: change is possible, but it takes informed and consistent decisions.

Urgency must come from understandable consequences, not fear tactics. Never manufacture panic, shame people for hardship or suggest that mindset alone can overcome structural barriers.

## Classical books, financial thinkers and public speakers

Books and talks may enrich a lesson when they clarify a useful money belief, behaviour or decision. They are supporting material, not authority by celebrity status.

- Use classical financial and personal-development books, biographies, academic works and reputable talks when the relevant idea is useful and accurately represented.
- Verify the author's identity, the original work, the exact passage or underlying idea, and a reliable source location. Do not repeat quote graphics or unattributed quote websites as evidence.
- Prefer an independently written paraphrase for modern copyrighted works. Attribute the idea to the author and name the book, speech or other original work where possible. Do not imitate distinctive wording or reconstruct a passage by swapping synonyms.
- Direct quotations from copyrighted material require documented permission or a compatible licence, or verified public-domain status for the relevant territory and use. A short quotation is not automatically lawful; attribution alone is not permission, and legal exceptions vary by jurisdiction.
- For direct quotations that pass rights review, apply a conservative 15-word internal limit per quote, preserve exact wording and punctuation, and identify the author and work. This internal limit is not a legal safe harbour.
- Public-domain status must be checked for the relevant country. A book's age, online availability or presence in a quote collection does not by itself establish that every edition, translation, recording or adaptation is public domain.
- Never invent, misattribute, or silently alter a quotation. If the original wording or source cannot be verified, omit the quote and express the general lesson without attribution.
- Use no more than one short literary or speaker reference in a short video unless the story genuinely needs more. The audience's practical lesson must remain central.

For modern speakers such as Zig Ziglar, default to a verified, independently worded summary of the idea and cite the original book or talk in the research record. Do not reproduce memorable lines from books or speeches merely because they are widely shared online.

## Emotional destination

A viewer should leave thinking:
- I recognise that behaviour or belief.
- I understand why it matters.
- I can see a different way to approach money.
- I know one useful step I can take.
- My circumstances may be difficult, but I am not powerless.

## Non-negotiable safeguards

- Never promise overnight riches, guaranteed wealth or guaranteed investment returns.
- Never shame, ridicule or blame people for poverty or financial hardship.
- Never imply that every financial problem can be solved by individual discipline alone.
- Keep the content educational and general, not personalised financial, investment, tax or legal advice.
- Include the required spoken, on-screen and description disclaimers.
- End with a realistic action appropriate to the lesson.

## Editorial test

Before a topic or script advances, ask:
1. Does it address a meaningful money mindset, behaviour or everyday decision?
2. Will the audience recognise themselves in the situation?
3. Is the lesson more important than the financial detail?
4. Is the explanation accurate without being unnecessarily technical?
5. Does the story create honest urgency and offer a practical response?
6. Does it leave viewers informed and hopeful rather than frightened or ashamed?

A topic should not be selected merely because a financial institution published something new. A strong evergreen lesson is more valuable than an irrelevant news headline.
